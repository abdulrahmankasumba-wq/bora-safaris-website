Beyond the Ordinary Game Drive: Lion Tracking in Queen Elizabeth National Park

There is a difference between going on a game drive and going out to track lions. On an ordinary game drive, you explore the savannah, searching for wildlife along the tracks and hoping that somewhere along the way, a lion will reveal itself. Lion tracking, however, takes you a little deeper into the world of these magnificent predators.

Your morning begins early in Queen Elizabeth National Park, when the savannah is still cool and the day is just beginning. You meet your ranger and, depending on the tracking team, researchers who are familiar with the lions living in the area. Before the search begins, you are introduced to the experience and the important work behind it. These are not simply lions being followed for entertainment. They are animals whose movements, behaviour and survival are being monitored to help protect their population.

You climb into the safari vehicle, and the search begins.

"These are not simply lions being followed for entertainment. They are animals whose movements, behaviour and survival are being monitored to help protect their population."

The Search Begins Across the Kasenyi Plains

The ranger may be communicating with the research team, checking information from previous sightings or using radio telemetry to locate a lion fitted with a tracking collar. As the vehicle moves through the Kasenyi Plains, your eyes are constantly searching. You look across the grass, beneath trees, around bushes and along the tracks. Every movement catches your attention. A Uganda kob lifting its head, a bird flying low across the grass or a patch of shade beneath a tree suddenly becomes worth examining.

Then the search begins to narrow.

The ranger slows down, listening and looking carefully. The tracking equipment begins helping the team establish the lion's location. You may find yourself leaning forward, trying to see through the grass before the ranger even says anything.

And Then, There It Is

Perhaps the lion is lying beneath a tree, resting after a night of hunting. Perhaps you find a female with other members of her pride, or a male lying quietly in the grass. There is no guarantee of what you will find because every tracking experience is different.

But when you finally see one, something changes. The excitement of the search gives way to the quiet wonder of being close to an animal that you have spent the morning trying to locate. You begin noticing its movements, its breathing, its ears responding to sounds around it, and the way it watches the world without appearing to be in a hurry.

Your ranger begins explaining what you are seeing. You may learn how researchers identify individual lions, why they monitor their movements, how prides interact, what threats they face, and why understanding their behaviour is important for conservation.

"For a moment, you are no longer simply a visitor taking photographs. You are witnessing a small part of the work that helps Uganda understand and protect its wildlife."

A Closer Connection to Conservation

And perhaps that is what makes lion tracking so special. You leave the plains with photographs, memories and a much deeper appreciation of the animal. You have not merely seen a lion. You have followed the story of its morning, understood a little of its world, and experienced the patience, knowledge and dedication involved in protecting it.

In Queen Elizabeth National Park, lion tracking turns a safari into something more personal. It brings you closer to the wilderness, closer to the work of conservation, and closer to understanding why these magnificent animals deserve to remain wild.
